## Why plan at all

A 2017 study of 40,554 adults in France found 57% planned their meals at least occasionally. Planners were more likely to be in the top quarter for diet quality (odds ratio 1.13) and for food variety (1.25). Women who planned had lower odds of overweight (0.92) and obesity (0.79); in men, only obesity was lower (0.81). The authors note the study can't show that planning caused these differences.

## The method

1. **Set your targets.** Work out daily calories and protein with our [macro calculator](/tools/macro-calculator) and [protein calculator](/tools/protein-calculator). Without numbers, a plan is just a list of recipes.
2. **Look at your week.** Mark late nights, training days and meals out. Busy nights get 15-minute dinners or leftovers; training days can carry more carbs.
3. **Repeat breakfasts and lunches.** Pick two or three of each and rotate them. Most people happily eat the same breakfast for a week.
4. **Plan dinners that share ingredients.** If Monday uses half a bag of spinach, put the other half in Wednesday's dinner. Cook once, eat twice: double Tuesday's dinner and it becomes Wednesday's lunch.
5. **Use snacks to close gaps.** Add up protein for each day. If you're short, a pot of Greek yogurt or a few boiled eggs fixes it.
6. **Build one grocery list.** Add up each ingredient across the week, check what's already in the cupboard, and sort the list by aisle.
7. **Prep and review.** Cook what you can ahead, keeping cooked food no more than 3 to 4 days in the fridge (see [how to meal prep for the week](/blog/how-to-meal-prep-for-the-week)). At the end of the week, drop what you didn't eat.
